01 · Find the money

Printed pp. 7–8. Gold → currency → deposits → securities is a historical teaching model. The money/credit boundary depends on the settlement problem. Modern reserve balances are distinct from notes and coins; modern fiat currency generally does not promise gold.

02 · Trace the promise

Printed p. 9. Matching claims cancel within a chosen consolidation boundary. Currency is outside the private sector but inside a boundary containing its issuer. Gold has no issuer’s liability. Our illustrative selected entries are an illustrative excerpt, not complete balance sheets; securities, loans, equity and other entries are omitted. Consolidation does not repay debts.

03 · Make room to trade

Printed pp. 11–12. Issuing a credible, accepted promise can relax today’s payment constraint. The issuer cannot create a higher layer’s settlement asset unilaterally. More credit is not automatically more settlement money.

04 · Expansion and contraction

Printed pp. 10–12. Separate credit quantity from credit’s moneyness. Counts and distances in the diagram are illustrative, not data, estimated elasticities or a calibrated crisis model.

05 · Connect the layers

Printed pp. 12–13. Dealers, banks and central banks connect layers through security prices/interest rates, par, and the gold exchange rate in the historical example. Supporting conversion can conceal qualitative differences until stress makes them visible.

06 · Manage the tension

Printed pp. 13–14. Policy can influence discipline and elasticity; longer rates need not follow the overnight rate mechanically. The reserve-loan diagram is a marked modern schematic extension. Liquidity support adds a repayment obligation and need not cure insolvency.

Beyond chapter 2 · Where deposits come from

The new loan example extends the chapter using Mehrling lectures 3–4, printed pp. 16–17 and 23. A borrower’s debt and a bank’s deposit promise are distinct contracts. The Bank of England’s explanation of money creation cross-checks this accounting and the limits to lending. The lesson does not imply unlimited creation or a fixed reserve multiplier.

Beyond chapter 2 · Paying across banks

Mehrling lectures 5–6, printed pp. 32–44, distinguish transfers, clearing, settlement and funding. Our picture isolates one gross payment with sufficient reserves. The BIS “Money and trust” section (2025) explains why the recipient holds a claim on its own bank. Moving markers represent accounting updates, not physical wallets travelling between banks.

Beyond chapter 2 · Sale is not repayment

Mehrling lectures 9–10, printed pp. 59–73. A dealer buys on its own account. The bond moves to a new holder while its issuer remains liable. Our single-payment $10 bond and $9/$8 quotes are invented examples. The conditional one-year return is payment minus price, divided by price. A promised return is not guaranteed.

Beyond chapter 2 · A problem of timing

Mehrling lecture 4, printed pp. 28–31, and lecture 12, pp. 81–91. Being able to make a payment today differs from having assets worth more than total debts. A liquidity loan can bridge timing; it need not repair losses. The café example assumes the future receipt and omits other debts. Central-bank lending is conditional; this lesson does not specify current facility rules.

Discipline and elasticity

Discipline and Elasticity, 00:10–03:30 and 04:55–08:35. An accepted promise can make trade possible now, while the deadline to deliver an acceptable payment remains a constraint. The currency and banking principles emphasize these different aspects. Neither is a complete account alone.

Borrowing for one night

Managing the Hierarchy, 08:15–09:35 introduces the overnight policy-sensitive end. Our original worked example adds the mechanics: an existing $100 reserve balance transfers from one bank to another and is repaid with $0.01 of interest. The assumed annual rate is 3.6% and the day-count basis is 360; these are illustrative, not current market quotes.

How policy reaches other rates

Managing the Hierarchy, 08:15–13:40 supplies the yield-curve question. The Federal Reserve’s educator primer supports the modern example of a lender comparing a market offer with central-bank remuneration. Actual market rates can diverge because access, risk and balance-sheet costs differ.

The Bank of England’s transmission explanation clarifies that future short-rate expectations, term premia and credit conditions affect longer rates. The professor’s critique of a strict expectations-only theory does not mean expectations are irrelevant. His inverted/ascending curves are illustrations, not universal policy rules. The final lecture question also discusses purchases of long-term securities; central-bank influence is not confined to the overnight rate.
