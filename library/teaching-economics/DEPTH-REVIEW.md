## Ten conceptual audits

1. **Unit versus instrument.** A dollar amount is a denomination, not an identity card for an
   instrument. A note, a deposit and a personal IOU can all say $5 while differing in issuer,
   access and use. The final café question tests whether the learner still equates equal labels.
2. **Payment versus postponement.** A personal IOU can enable lunch without extinguishing the
   debt. Add a next-day repayment that exchanges the IOU for cash. Do not imply personal debt
   survives actual repayment or that accepting a promise is the same event as paying it.
3. **Creation versus transfer.** A same-bank purchase reallocates existing deposits. A loan can
   add a deposit and a distinct repayment obligation. Show two promises with different directions
   and dates; do not depict money as a bank handing over a saver's wallet. The new loan example
   extends chapter two using lectures three and four; it is explicitly marked as such.
4. **Customer settlement versus bank settlement.** Retain the same café purchase when changing
   banks. Show customer balances and the banks' central-bank balances separately. Transfer
   conserves total deposits and reserves in this isolated example. Deposits do not transform
   into reserves, nor does the café acquire a claim on the payer's bank. Gross transfer is one
   chosen case, not the universal architecture: netting, correspondents and intraday credit exist.
5. **Gross obligations versus consolidated net.** Combining a holder and issuer's accounts
   makes their claim net out. It neither transfers funds nor releases the issuer. Gross promises
   retain dates and counterparties; zero net financial wealth is not zero goods or zero activity.
   Keep the original entries visible inside a boundary; never animate debts disappearing here.
6. **Scarcity versus elasticity.** A participant may issue its own accepted promise but not simply
   write itself an asset issued by someone else. Credit limits and access to funding matter.
   This does not imply a universally fixed supply of reserves, nor an exogenous deposit multiple.
   Include limits in optional explanation, not a new macroeconomics course on the main screen.
7. **Quantity versus moneyness.** Four unchanged IOUs can become unusable for a particular urgent
   payment without default or a change in face amount. Replace vague “feels riskier” with a
   supplier requiring payment today. The new setting changes acceptance needs; it is not a
   calibrated model of crisis, credit spreads or the business cycle.
8. **Conversion versus redemption.** Withdrawing reduces a deposit and delivers currency at par.
   Selling a bond transfers an existing claim to a buyer; it does not repay the issuer's debt.
   Show the dealer's payment as well as the bond. A one-year $10 claim offered at $9 or $8 makes
   price and conditional return concrete. The higher yield is conditional on repayment, not
   guaranteed profit. Dealers take positions and require funding; they are not frictionless pipes.
9. **Liquidity versus solvency.** $10 expected tomorrow cannot by itself settle $5 due today.
   A bridge loan may solve timing without repairing asset losses. Forced sales and funding costs
   can connect the two problems. Introduce timing with the café before moving to a bank's
   reserves. Do not infer solvency from one expected receipt; other obligations are omitted.
10. **Backstop versus omnipotence.** Central-bank lending creates its own liabilities under
    institutional conditions; it cannot create goods or another issuer's currency by bookkeeping.
    Policy influences funding costs without dictating all risk assessments or term rates. A
    concrete business-loan question replaces the rote “rates move exactly together” question.

## Worked-account checks behind the drawings

- Lunch cash: customer −$5, café +$5; debt for this purchase extinguished.
- Accepted personal IOU: café claim +$5, customer debt +$5; cash unchanged.
- IOU repaid: café claim −$5 and cash +$5; customer cash −$5 and debt −$5.
- Loan created: borrower deposit asset +$5 / loan liability +$5; bank loan asset +$5 /
  deposit liability +$5. Two distinct contracts, four entries, no new net wealth at origination.
- Same-bank purchase: customer's deposit −$5, café deposit +$5; total bank deposit liability unchanged.
- Cross-bank purchase: customer $5→$0, café $0→$5; A reserves $20→$15, B $20→$25.
  The bank-account balances are illustrative starting amounts, not reserve requirements.
- Withdrawal: customer deposit $5→$0, cash $0→$5. Bank deposit liability and cash asset each −$5.
- Bond sale: seller loses claim and gains $9; dealer gains claim and pays $9. Issuer still owes $10.
  If a single $10 payment occurs in one year, ($10−$9)/$9 ≈ 11.1%; at $8 the conditional return is 25%.
- Central-bank loan: bank gains reserves and owes a loan; central bank gains a loan claim and
  owes reserves. A different transaction from a private bank manufacturing reserves itself.

## Teaching decisions

Keep the accepted everyday opening and 25 short manual screens. Extra depth belongs in
optional explanation, this teacher record and source links. No derivatives, reserve-ratio
arithmetic, repo haircut calculations, FX parity formulas, legal rules or historical rate
numbers enter the novice path. Their value here is to prevent false generalisations.

Do not use a universal gold→currency→deposit→bond ladder to describe every modern system.
Historical screens are explicitly marked. Preserve asset identities, fixed-size and fixed-speed
moving markers, non-overlapping paths, and Wink beside the focus. Bookkeeping markers are
not literal physical objects. The bond's two legs are separated vertically for visibility, not
presented as a required settlement timing protocol.

Question feedback is formative and not recorded as mastery. User feedback, rather than a
passing test suite, is still needed to assess whether these explanations teach well.
